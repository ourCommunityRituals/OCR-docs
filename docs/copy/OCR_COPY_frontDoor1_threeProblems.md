## 1. The one decision this document needs

**Hero hook order.** The research ranks surveillance as the hook, connection as
the promise, business as the proof. OCR-116 orders the *problems* connection →
business → surveillance.

**These are not in conflict, and the draft below does both.** The hero leads on
surveillance-relief, because it is the sharpest thing we can say and the one
nobody else can say honestly. The problem section then starts where the visitor
already is — "there's nothing to do around here" — and climbs to the problem
they have not yet put into words. The relief then lands on the problem the hero
already promised, which closes the loop instead of repeating it.

Three hero variants are drafted in §3. **Pick one.** Everything downstream —
design, deck, the Stage-1 benchmark — keys off that choice.

## 3. Hero — three variants

All three are anti-promises, not claims. All three avoid "impression", "reach",
"turnout", "eyeballs". None uses "flywheel".

### Variant A — the anti-promise (recommended)

> # We count the room. We never count you.
>
> Community events, run on a platform that was built so it *cannot* know who
> showed up. Not a policy. Not a setting you have to find. The architecture.
>
> **[See what's happening near you]**   [How this works]

*Why A:* leads with the thing no competitor can say without lying, and says it
in seven words. "Cannot" is load-bearing — it is the difference between a
promise and a property. Reads on a 390px screen without wrapping past two lines.

### Variant B — the question

> # Who's counting you tonight?
>
> Every app at every door wants a name, a number, a login. Ours doesn't. We
> track that a group gathered, here, when — and nothing else, because there is
> nothing else to take.
>
> **[See what's happening near you]**   [How this works]

*Why B:* higher tension, better for paid acquisition. Risk: opens on an
accusation, which is a colder first note than the brand's warm register.

### Variant C — the local lead

> # Your neighborhood, visible to itself.
>
> The block party, the league night, the back room at the bar. All of it in one
> place — counted honestly, and never tied to your name.
>
> **[See what's happening near you]**   [How this works]

*Why C:* warmest, most on-brand, and the safest. Risk: it is the line a dozen
other event platforms could also run. The privacy clause arrives as a
subordinate clause instead of the point.

**Recommendation: A.** B is the better ad; C is the better neighbor; A is the
only one that is both differentiated and true on first read. If the Stage-1
benchmark runs privacy-led vs local-led, A and C are the two arms.

## 4. Problem 1 — "There's nothing to do around here"

> ## "There's nothing to do around here."
>
> There is. You just can't see it.
>
> The Tuesday bocce league. The back-room open mic. The block party three
> streets over that forty of your neighbors already knew about. None of it is
> hidden — it's just scattered across six group chats, a flyer in a window, and
> somebody's cousin's story.
>
> Every neighborhood already has a full calendar. Almost none of them have a way
> to see it.

**Notes.** No product yet. The turn inside the section is "there is — you just
can't see it," which reframes scarcity as invisibility. Specifics are real
things a real bar owner would name, per voice rule. Stays under one screen at
390px.

---

## 5. Problem 2 — "The little guys got priced out"

> ## "The little guys got priced out."
>
> The barber two doors down would sponsor the league. He'd put up forty dollars
> and a stack of cards, and he'd mean it.
>
> But the platforms that sell local advertising don't sell *local*. They sell an
> auction, and the auction is open to anyone with a bigger budget and no stake in
> the block. A chain three states away outbids the barber for the corner he
> actually stands on.
>
> Scale was supposed to make advertising cheaper. For the smallest advertiser on
> the street, it did the opposite.

**Notes.** Names a person, not a segment. "Forty dollars and a stack of cards"
is the specificity rule doing work. No mechanism — the 30% local floor, the
7×/3×/1× weights and locals-first ordering are *not* mentioned here; they belong
in the trust section or settings. The claim made is about the auction, which is
true and checkable.

---

## 6. Problem 3 — "Everything watches you now"

> ## "Everything watches you now."
>
> The loyalty app wants your number. The door wants your email. The camera over
> the bar is doing something with your face that nobody at the bar can explain to
> you. The pixel on the ticket page followed you home.
>
> None of this asked you a real question. "Accept all" is not a choice — it's a
> door you can't get through any other way.
>
> Illinois already decided this was a problem. **BIPA** — the Biometric
> Information Privacy Act — is the strongest biometric privacy law in the
> country, and it has been on the books here since 2008. It gives you the right
> to sue, personally, if someone takes your faceprint without written consent.
>
> The strongest privacy law in America is home turf. Most of what gets built here
> still acts like it isn't.

**Notes.** Concrete before legal — four things they have personally seen, then
the statute. BIPA framed as *home turf*, per the ticket. The "Accept all is not
a choice" line is the hinge into §7 and should be the last thing they read
before the turn. No case citations, no dates beyond 2008 — this is a pitch page,
not a brief.

## 7. The turn — The Choice

> ## Never who. Always how many.
>
> Here's the whole idea.
>
> A poster goes up in a window. Someone scans it. We learn that **a scan happened
> at that corner, in that hour** — and that is the end of the list. No name. No
> number. No email. No face. Not stored somewhere safer; **never collected.**
>
> That isn't a privacy policy. It's the shape of the database. There is no column
> for your name because there is no way to put one there — the tests that run on
> every change to this platform fail if anyone tries.
>
> So the host learns the room filled. The barber learns his corner worked. The
> block learns what it looks like when it shows up.
>
> And you stay a stranger, on purpose.
>
> **[See what's happening near you]**

**Notes — this is the load-bearing section.**

- "Never collected" beats "safely stored," and it is literally true: two
  databases, no cross-linking, no PII column on the anonymous side.
- "The tests fail if anyone tries" is the honest way to say *anonymous by
  architecture, not by policy* without saying "CI invariant gate" to a stranger.
  It is also verifiable, which matters — every claim on this page has to survive
  someone checking it.
- Three beneficiaries in one breath (host, barber, block) pay off problems 1 and
  2 without a product section.
- "You stay a stranger, on purpose" is the line to put on the poster.

## 9. What is deliberately absent

Named here so a reviewer doesn't read the omissions as gaps:

- **No mechanism.** No weights, no tiers, no floors, no depth tables, no
  "flywheel." OSE-1011 puts those behind a single *More* control.
- **No counts.** No "500 events" or "12 neighborhoods" — the honesty lexicon
  bars count claims, and we do not have honest numbers to quote yet anyway.
- **No product screenshots above the relief.** Problems before product.
- **No "47 event types" in the problem sections.** Breadth is section 7's job;
  leading with it makes the page about us.
- **No competitor named.** The auction critique is structural, not a callout.
